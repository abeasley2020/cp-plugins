:::problem The same gloves, back on the line
A line lead at a sandwich plant watches a worker empty the trash bin and walk straight back to the assembly table in the same gloves. Stopping the line costs a few minutes of output. Letting it go risks moving whatever was on that bin lid onto ready-to-eat food, and a recall caused by a hand-borne pathogen costs far more than a short stop. The lead has to decide now, and so will you on your own lines.
:::

## Hands are the shortest route to the food

Ready-to-eat food gets no further cooking step to kill what lands on it. That makes the worker's hands the most common route for moving pathogens from a person, a surface or raw product onto the food. **Cross-contact by hand** happens without anyone seeing it, which is why plants rely on rules about when to wash rather than on judging whether hands look clean.

The layout of a line shapes whether those rules hold. A sink that is hard to reach does not get used, however good the policy is.

Look at where the hand sink sits on this line, and at the path the worker takes from the trash bin.

## Five moments that call for a wash

The source lists the moments when a wash is required. Each one marks a change in what the hands have touched.

- **Entering the production area.** Hands carry whatever was touched outside: door handles, phones, lockers. The wash at the door resets them.
- **After the restroom.** This is the moment most people remember, and the one where a missed wash does the most harm.
- **After touching the face or hair.** Faces and hair carry their own bacteria. A quick touch to adjust a hairnet counts.
- **After raw product or trash.** This is the opening case. Raw product and waste are the dirtiest things on most lines.
- **When changing tasks.** Moving from one job to another, such as from packing to assembly, changes what the hands will touch next.

:::term Handwash
Wet with warm running water, apply soap, scrub every surface for at least 10 to 15 seconds, rinse, and dry with a single-use towel. The whole wash takes about 20 seconds.
:::

The scrub covers the backs of the hands, between the fingers and under the nails, which are the places a quick rinse misses.

## Gloves and sanitizer come after the wash, not instead of it

Gloves feel like protection, and that feeling is the problem. A glove picks up whatever it touches just as a bare hand does, so a worker who empties the trash in gloves has dirty gloves. The rule in the source is plain: wash before putting gloves on, and change gloves at every moment that calls for a wash.

Sanitizer has a narrower job than most people think. It is used after washing, not in place of it, and it works poorly on hands that are wet, greasy or soiled. On a sandwich line, hands are often all three.

:::compare What each one does
### A handwash
Removes soil, grease and most of what the hands picked up. Required at each of the five moments.

### Gloves
Keep bare skin off the food. Only as clean as the last thing they touched, so they are changed at each of the five moments.

### Sanitizer
Reduces what is left on clean, dry hands. Never replaces the wash.
:::

## Check your understanding

:::scenario objective=o3.1 bloom=apply source="week3-notes.md slide 8"
You are the line lead in the opening case. The worker is back at the table in the same gloves. What do you do?
- [ ] Let it go this once, since the shift is behind.
  Result: The line keeps moving, but whatever was on the bin lid may now be on ready-to-eat food. The cost of being wrong is a recall.
- [x] Stop the worker, have them remove the gloves, wash and put on new gloves.
  Result: About a minute lost. The worker's hands and gloves are reset before they touch food.
- [ ] Hand them sanitizer and let them carry on in the same gloves.
  Result: Sanitizer on soiled gloves does little. The gloves still carry what they picked up.
:::

:::check objective=o3.2 bloom=understand source="week3-notes.md slide 6"
Why does a worker need to wash before putting on gloves?
- [ ] Gloves stick to wet hands, so washing makes them easier to put on.
  Why: Comfort is not the reason. The rule is about what the hands carry.
- [x] Gloves pick up whatever the hands carried when they were put on, so dirty hands make dirty gloves.
  Why: Right. A glove is only as clean as the hands that put it on and the last thing it touched.
- [ ] Washing is only needed if the gloves will be worn for more than an hour.
  Why: Time is not in the rule. The wash is tied to what the hands touched, not how long gloves are worn.
:::

:::check objective=o3.1 bloom=apply spaced_from=m01 source="week3-notes.md slide 6"
A worker on the packing table is moved to sandwich assembly halfway through a shift. Which moments call for a wash? Choose all that apply.
- [x] Changing tasks from packing to assembly.
  Why: A change of task is one of the five moments, because the hands will touch different things next.
- [x] Before putting on fresh gloves for assembly.
  Why: Gloves go on clean hands, so the wash comes first.
- [ ] Only at the end of the shift.
  Why: Waiting means every surface the hands touched in between moves to the food.
:::

:::recall objective=o3.2 bloom=understand source="week3-notes.md slides 6 and 7"
In two or three sentences, explain to a new hire why neither gloves nor sanitizer replace a handwash.
Model answer: Gloves pick up whatever they touch, so they are only as clean as the hands that put them on and the last thing they handled. Sanitizer works poorly on wet, greasy or soiled hands, which describes most hands on a line. Only a full wash removes the soil, so it comes first and the gloves and sanitizer come after.
Criteria: names that gloves carry contamination, names the limit of sanitizer, and puts the wash first.
:::
